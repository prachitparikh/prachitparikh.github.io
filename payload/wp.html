"The system must be fast." Plenty of requirements documents have that sentence in them, usually in a section near the end called Non-Functional Requirements, usually written the week before sign-off, usually by whoever drew the short straw.

Then UAT starts. A tester submits a return on the new portal, waits four seconds, and logs a defect: "too slow." The developer replies that it's fast. Both of them are right, because nobody ever wrote down what fast means, measured how, for whom, under what load.

This is a long practical guide to fixing that. You'll get a template that turns any quality wish into a test, a list of the vague words I rewrite on sight, a filled catalog for a fictional returns portal (including the AI feature on it), and a small SQL fixture you can run yourself that shows why "average response time under 500 ms" is a requirement that can pass while users suffer. The example client is fictional: Northstar Systems, which I use for every example on this blog.

Prefer to watch? The same idea for functional requirements, how to write criteria a tester can't misread, is in my video How to Write Acceptance Criteria Testers Can't Misread. Non-functional requirements follow the same rule: if a stranger couldn't write the test, it isn't done.

What non-functional requirements are (the short version)

A functional requirement says what the system does: "A customer can request a refund for an order delivered in the last 30 days." A non-functional requirement says how well it does it, or under what constraints: how fast the request goes through, how many customers can do it at once, who can see the refund history, what happens when the payment service is down.

People also call them quality attributes, quality requirements or "the -ilities". The names matter less than the habit. The functional requirement gets a user story, acceptance criteria and a test. The non-functional one gets a sentence. That's the gap this guide closes.

Why this matters more this year

Two reasons, one old and one new.

The old one: there's a current international standard for what "quality" even covers, and most requirements templates I see predate its latest version. ISO/IEC 25010, the product quality model, was revised in November 2023. The ISO listing says the model "is composed of nine characteristics" (the 2011 version had eight). According to arc42's summary of the update, safety was added as its own characteristic, usability was renamed interaction capability, portability was renamed flexibility, and scalability now sits under flexibility as a sub-characteristic. If your NFR template still has a "Portability" heading and no "Safety" heading, it's using the 2011 map.

The new one: AI features. A returns portal in 2026 might classify the customer's free-text reason with a model, suggest a refund amount, or summarize a case for an agent. That feature has quality requirements nobody had to write ten years ago. What's the accuracy bar? What happens when the model provider is down? What does each call cost, and what's the monthly ceiling? These are non-functional requirements, and if the BA doesn't write them, they get decided by default in a config file.

Who searches for this

If you've typed "non-functional requirements examples", "how to write NFRs", "measurable non-functional requirements" or "NFR template" into a search bar, you're in the right place. So are testers who keep inheriting "the system shall be user-friendly" and want something to send back.

The take: most NFRs shouldn't be in a separate section at all

Here's where I part ways with a lot of templates. I think the separate "Non-Functional Requirements" section at the end of a requirements document is where quality goes to die.

It's written last, by one person, in general terms, and it applies to "the system". Nobody owns it. Nobody tests it until performance testing, if there is any. And because it's general, it's vague: "the system shall be available 99.9% of the time" says nothing about which part, which hours, or what counts as available.

What works better: attach each quality requirement to the user story or feature it's about, with its own acceptance criteria and its own test, exactly like a functional one. "Submit a return" gets a response-time requirement. "View refund history" gets an access requirement. The AI reason classifier gets an accuracy requirement and a fallback requirement. A short system-wide list remains (things like data retention and browser support that genuinely apply everywhere), but it's short.

You might disagree. Architects often prefer one place to see every quality attribute, and that's a fair need. My answer is that a catalog view (like the one later in this post) can be built from the story-level requirements, but you can't build story-level tests from a general paragraph. Write them where they're tested. Roll them up for the architect.

The anatomy of a testable NFR

Every non-functional requirement I write has the same eight parts. It looks like a lot. Most of them are one line.

Part What it answers Example (submit a return)
ID How do we trace it? NFR-PERF-01, linked to story RET-12
Quality Which ISO/IEC 25010 characteristic? Performance efficiency (time behavior)
Scope Which feature, user, or path? Customer submits a return on the portal, web and mobile
Measure What exactly is counted? Time from tapping Submit to the confirmation screen, measured at the server
Target What number, at what percentile? 95% of submits complete in 1,000 ms or less
Conditions Under what load, data, time window? Up to 200 concurrent submits; any 28-day window
Fail line What's clearly unacceptable? Any 1-hour window where fewer than 90% complete in 1,000 ms
Owner and test Who signs, how is it checked? Product owner, returns; load test LT-03 plus production monitoring

Four of those parts do most of the work.

Measure removes the argument about what's being timed. "Response time" can mean the server's processing time, the time until the page starts drawing, or the time until the confirmation appears. Each is a different number. Pick one and write it down.

Target with a percentile removes the averaging trap (more on this in the SQL section). Google's Site Reliability Engineering book is blunt about it: "We generally prefer to work with percentiles rather than the mean (arithmetic average) of a set of values." The SRE book's chapter on service level objectives explains why: "it's entirely possible for most of the requests to be fast, but for a long tail of requests to be much, much slower."

Conditions stop the requirement from being met in a quiet test environment and failed in production on a Monday morning. A response time with no load attached isn't a requirement. It's a hope.

Fail line is the one most templates skip, and it's the one stakeholders understand fastest. The target is what you aim for. The fail line is what makes you stop a release or open an incident. Having both lets people disagree about the target without disagreeing about what's broken.

Borrowing from SRE: SLIs, SLOs and error budgets for BAs

Site reliability engineers solved a version of this problem years ago, and their vocabulary is worth stealing.

The SRE Workbook describes a service level indicator as "an indicator of the level of service that you are providing", and recommends "treating the SLI as the ratio of two numbers: the number of good events divided by the total number of events." A service level objective then sets a target for that ratio. And the error budget is simple: "the SLO is a target percentage and the error budget is 100% minus the SLO."

Translate that into requirements language and you get a pattern that fits almost any quality attribute:

[Percentage] of [events] must be [good, defined precisely] over [time window], measured by [method].

So "the portal must be fast" becomes "99% of return submissions must complete in 1,000 ms or less over any 28-day window, measured at the server." Now you can do arithmetic with it. If the portal handles 200,000 submissions in 28 days, the error budget is 1%, which is 2,000 slow submissions. Spend them on a bad deploy and you know you're out. That's a conversation a product owner can have, which is the point.

The ratio pattern works well beyond speed. Availability: good events are successful requests. Data freshness: good events are reports whose data is less than an hour old. AI accuracy: good events are classifications a reviewer agrees with. One pattern, one test shape, every time.

The vague-word list

These are the words I rewrite on sight in any NFR. Each one hides a decision someone hasn't made yet.

If the requirement says Ask Rewrite as (example)
fast, quick, responsive Which action, measured where, at what percentile? 95% of return submits complete in 1,000 ms or less, measured at the server
scalable From what load to what load, by when? Handles 200 concurrent submits at launch and 600 by next peak season with the same response target
available 24/7, always up Which service, which hours, what counts as down? Returns API answers 99.5% of health checks successfully per calendar month, excluding announced maintenance up to 4 hours
secure Against what, for which data, who may do what? Only the customer and returns agents can view a refund's bank details; every view is logged with user and time
user-friendly, intuitive Which users, doing what task, measured how? 8 of 10 first-time test users submit a return without help in under 3 minutes
robust, reliable Which failure, what should happen? If the payment service doesn't respond in 10 seconds, the return is saved as Pending and the customer sees "We've got your return"
accessible To what standard and which screens? Return flow screens meet the team's accessibility checklist; tested with a screen reader and keyboard only
accurate (for AI features) Agreeing with whom, on what sample, how often? On the held-out set of 400 labelled reasons, the classifier matches the reviewer on at least 90%

Notice that every rewrite has a number someone has to choose. That's deliberate. The BA's job isn't to invent the number. It's to make sure somebody with the authority chooses it, out loud, before the build.

And when nobody knows the right number yet? Write the requirement with "TBD by [name], [date]" in the target field and put it in your assumptions log. A visible TBD beats an invisible "fast" every time, because a TBD has an owner and a deadline.

The SQL fixture: why "average under 500 ms" is a trap

This is the part I'd most like you to run yourself, because it changes how people read NFRs.

Picture a stakeholder who writes: "Average response time for return submission must be under 500 ms." It sounds strict. Here's a fictional log of 1,000 submissions to show what it allows.

First, make the data. This Python script builds a SQLite table where most submissions take 120 to 320 ms and 6% hit a slow path (say, a fraud check timing out) and take 3.2 to 4.8 seconds:

import random, sqlite3

random.seed(48)
con = sqlite3.connect("portal.db")
con.execute("DROP TABLE IF EXISTS request_log")
con.execute("""CREATE TABLE request_log (
    request_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, endpoint TEXT,
    duration_ms INTEGER, status INTEGER)""")
rows = []
for i in range(1, 1001):
    slow = i % 100 in (7, 19, 33, 48, 61, 74)   # 6 in every 100
    d = random.randint(3200, 4800) if slow else random.randint(120, 320)
    rows.append((i, "/returns/submit", d, 200))
con.executemany("INSERT INTO request_log VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?)", rows)
con.commit()

Now check the stakeholder's requirement:

SELECT COUNT(*) AS requests, ROUND(AVG(duration_ms)) AS avg_ms
FROM request_log;

On my run that returns 1,000 requests and an average of 450 ms. The requirement passes.

Now look at the 95th percentile (nearest-rank method: sort, take the 950th value):

SELECT duration_ms AS p95_ms
FROM request_log
ORDER BY duration_ms
LIMIT 1 OFFSET (SELECT CAST(0.95 * COUNT(*) AS INT) - 1 FROM request_log);

That returns 3,495 ms. The median (change 0.95 to 0.50) is 226 ms. So the typical customer gets a fast portal, and about one in seventeen waits more than three seconds. The average sits in the middle and describes nobody.

Finally, the SLO-style version of the requirement:

SELECT COUNT(*) AS requests,
       SUM(CASE WHEN duration_ms <= 1000 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS good,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN duration_ms <= 1000 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
             / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct_good
FROM request_log;

940 of 1,000 are good: 94.0%. Against "95% of submits in 1,000 ms or less", that's a fail, and it should be. The same log, the same users, opposite verdicts, depending only on how the requirement was written.

This is the SRE book's point in one table. Their own example: "although a typical request is served in about 50 ms, 5% of requests are 20 times slower!" A requirement written as an average can't see that. A percentile or a good-events ratio can.

Two notes before you take this to a performance tester. Percentile functions differ between databases (PostgreSQL has percentile_cont, others have their own), and different methods give slightly different answers on small samples. Say which method the test uses. And a percentile over the whole month can hide one terrible hour, which is what the fail line is for.

On a skewed distribution the mean drifts toward the tail while the median stays with the typical value, which is exactly what happens to response times. Image: "Comparison mean median mode.svg" by Cmglee, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

A filled NFR catalog: the Northstar returns portal

Here's a catalog for the fictional Northstar returns portal, organized by the 2023 ISO/IEC 25010 characteristics. Each row is attached to a story, so it rolls up from the backlog rather than living on its own. All numbers are examples a product owner would choose, not recommendations.

ID Quality (ISO 25010:2023) Story Requirement Fail line Test
NFR-PERF-01 Performance efficiency RET-12 Submit a return 95% of submits complete in 1,000 ms or less at the server, at up to 200 concurrent submits, any 28-day window Under 90% in any 1-hour window Load test LT-03; production monitoring
NFR-PERF-02 Performance efficiency RET-20 Agent case view Case view loads in 2 s or less for 95% of opens during 9 AM-6 PM ET Under 85% on any business day Synthetic check every 5 min
NFR-CAP-01 Flexibility (scalability) RET-12 NFR-PERF-01 still holds at 600 concurrent submits Target missed at 400 Load test LT-04 before peak season
NFR-AVL-01 Reliability (availability) All return APIs 99.5% of health checks succeed per calendar month, excluding announced maintenance up to 4 h Below 99.0% Uptime monitor, monthly report
NFR-REC-01 Reliability (recoverability) RET-12 If the payment service doesn't answer in 10 s, the return saves as Pending and is retried within 15 min; no return is lost Any submitted return missing from the database Kill-the-dependency test in UAT; daily SQL count match
NFR-SEC-01 Security (confidentiality) RET-31 Refund history Bank details visible only to the customer and agents with the Returns role; every view logged with user ID and time Any view by another role Role-based UAT cases; log review
NFR-SEC-02 Security (accountability) RET-33 Agent overrides refund Every override records agent, old amount, new amount, reason, time; logs kept 7 years Override without a log row SQL: overrides without audit rows = 0
NFR-INT-01 Interaction capability RET-12 8 of 10 first-time test users submit a return without help in under 3 minutes Fewer than 6 of 10 Moderated usability test, 10 users
NFR-SAF-01 Safety RET-40 Hazardous item return Returns flagged as hazardous (batteries, chemicals) never show the drop-off option; the customer sees collection instructions Drop-off option shown for any flagged item UAT cases for each hazard category
NFR-MNT-01 Maintainability (testability) All Every refund rule has an automated test referencing its rule ID Any rule ID with no test Traceability matrix check

The safety row is new for a lot of teams, and it's one reason to update to the 2023 model. In the 2011 version there was nowhere obvious to put "the system must never offer a drop-off for a battery return". It isn't security, it isn't usability. Under 25010:2023 it has a home, and once it has a home it gets written.

NFRs for the AI feature

Northstar's portal has one AI feature: a classifier that reads the customer's free-text return reason ("arrived cracked", "wrong size, my fault") and sorts it into a reason code that drives the refund rule. Here are the quality requirements it needs. None of them are exotic. They're the same eight-part shape.

ID Quality Requirement Fail line Test
NFR-AI-01 Functional suitability (correctness) On a held-out set of 400 labelled reasons, the classifier matches the reviewer's code on at least 90%, and on at least 97% for the two codes that trigger a full refund Below 85% overall or below 95% on full-refund codes Run the held-out set before release and after any model or prompt change
NFR-AI-02 Reliability (fault tolerance) If the model doesn't answer in 3 s or returns no valid code, the return goes to the "Needs review" queue with no reason code; the customer flow doesn't stop Any customer sees an error because the classifier failed Kill-the-model test in UAT
NFR-AI-03 Performance efficiency (resource utilization) Average model cost per return stays under a ceiling the product owner sets; monthly spend alerts at 80% of budget Spend over budget with no alert Monthly cost report vs return count
NFR-AI-04 Security (accountability) Every classification stores the input text, model version, output code and time Any classified return with no stored model version SQL: classified returns without model_version = 0
NFR-AI-05 Maintainability (testability) A model or prompt change is a change request that reruns NFR-AI-01 before release Model version changed in production with no rerun record Release checklist

Two of these deserve a word.

NFR-AI-01 has two thresholds, overall and on the codes that cost money. A classifier that's 92% accurate overall but weak on the "damaged on arrival" code, the one that triggers a full refund, is a finance problem dressed up as a good score. Split the bar by consequence.

NFR-AI-02 is the one teams forget. The AI feature fails gracefully or it fails in front of the customer. Write which one you want.

And on sample size: 400 held-out cases sounds like a lot until you ask how confident you can be about the rare codes. If a code appears 20 times in the set, a perfect score on it still leaves real room for error. I went through that arithmetic in a post on AI UAT sample sizes; it applies here too.

Where NFRs go in the traceability matrix

An NFR that isn't traced doesn't get tested. Each one needs a row like any other requirement: ID, the story it's attached to, the test that proves it, the result, and who signed.

The V-model pairs each level of definition on the left with a level of testing on the right. Non-functional requirements belong on the left side too, each with its partner test on the right. Image: "V-model.svg" by Herman Bruyninckx, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The pairing in that diagram is the whole argument. Functional requirements get matched with tests as a matter of course. NFRs often get matched with "performance testing (TBD)". Give each one a named test with an ID, and put it in the matrix. If you use the traceability matrix template in my BA Toolkit, NFRs go in the same sheet with a type column, not a separate tab nobody opens.

How to run an NFR workshop in 45 minutes

The fastest way I know to get real numbers is a short, focused session with the product owner, a developer or architect, and a tester. Bring the vague-word list and the eight-part template.

  1. 10 minutes: pick the five stories that matter most. Usually the ones customers touch most, the ones that move money, and anything with an AI step.
  2. 15 minutes: for each story, ask the four questions. How fast, for whom? What happens when a dependency fails? Who can see or change what? What would make you stop a release?
  3. 10 minutes: write targets and fail lines. Get a number, even a provisional one. Mark provisional ones TBD with an owner and a date.
  4. 10 minutes: name the test for each. If nobody can say how it'll be tested, the requirement goes back for rewording, not into the document.

Questions that get past "fast" quickly:

  • "If this took four seconds for one customer in twenty, would you hold the release?"
  • "If the payment provider went down for ten minutes, what should a customer see?"
  • "Who, by job title, should never see this field?"
  • "If the AI got this wrong once in fifty, what would it cost us?"

Numbers come out of questions like these because they're about consequences, which stakeholders know, rather than milliseconds, which they don't.

Mistakes I see most

Writing NFRs about "the system". The system is a dozen services with different users and different stakes. Scope every NFR to a feature or a path.

Copying numbers from another project. "99.9% availability" from the last project's document is meaningless without knowing what was measured there. Choose your own, from consequences.

No conditions. A response time without a load is met in the test lab and failed on launch day.

Untestable verbs. "Support", "handle", "allow for" and "be capable of" all need a measure behind them.

Treating security as one NFR. "The system shall be secure" is a category, not a requirement. Break it into who can see what, who can change what, and what gets logged.

Forgetting the AI feature has a fallback. If you write one AI NFR, write NFR-AI-02.

What I'd do on Monday

  1. Open your current requirements document and search for the words in the vague-word list. Count them.
  2. Pick the three NFRs attached to the most-used customer action and rewrite each in the eight-part shape.
  3. For any performance NFR written as an average, rewrite it as a percentile or a good-events ratio. Run the fixture above if you need to convince someone.
  4. Check your template's headings against the nine ISO/IEC 25010:2023 characteristics. Add Safety if it's missing.
  5. If there's an AI feature in scope, write at least NFR-AI-01 (accuracy with a split bar) and NFR-AI-02 (fallback).
  6. Add every NFR to the traceability matrix with a test ID. Anything without a test ID goes on the agenda for the 45-minute workshop.
